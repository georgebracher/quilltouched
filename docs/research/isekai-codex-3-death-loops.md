# Everquill — Death, Loops, and Lethality With Emotional Weight

## Core thesis

Everquill should not feel like a game about repeatedly replacing failed characters. It should feel like a game about **one world accumulating the cost of many singular lives**.

The productive shape is a spiral, not a loop:

> **A life creates attachments → risk tests them → death converts them into consequences → the world remembers imperfectly → an heir enters at a changed narrative altitude.**

Repeated death remains meaningful when it preserves four things:

1. **Causality:** the player understands why the character died.
2. **Singularity:** the heir cannot reproduce the dead character’s relationships, perspective, or social place.
3. **Persistence:** death changes the world rather than resetting it.
4. **Forward agency:** the player quickly receives meaningful new choices rather than being sent back through the same grind.

A useful design heuristic is:

> **Productive death ≈ attachment × fairness × persistence × specificity × forward agency**  
> **Punishing death ≈ lost time × opacity × repetition × helplessness**

Research on permadeath supports this distinction. Permadeath reliably increases frustration, but assigning meaning to the death can turn that frustration into motivation to continue. Studies of *DayZ* similarly distinguish memorable deaths with legible causes and narrative context from “bad deaths” caused by bugs, incomprehensible systems, or attacks that erase agency. [[NCCU study](https://ah.lib.nccu.edu.tw/item?item_id=178951); [Fear, Loss and Meaningful Play](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320646682_Fear_loss_and_meaningful_play_Permadeath_in_DayZ)]

## Lessons from the reference works

### Re:Zero: death grows heavier because experience does not reset

*Return by Death* appears structurally capable of cheapening death: Subaru dies, time rewinds, and lost people can potentially be saved. It instead intensifies the stakes because the reset is radically asymmetric.

- **The world resets; Subaru does not.** Pain, terror, guilt, and knowledge accumulate in one consciousness. Later danger is frightening precisely because he knows what dying feels like.
- **Knowledge is the practical inheritance.** Subaru gains no conventional combat dominance from a failed timeline. He carries clues, schedules, enemy methods, and knowledge of people’s hidden selves.
- **Relationships do not carry forward.** Subaru remembers intimacy, confessions, betrayals, and deaths that the other person never experienced. He must re-earn trust while privately grieving a version of that relationship that has ceased to exist.
- **Success contains invisible casualties.** A “perfect” surviving timeline is emotionally built from failed versions that only Subaru can remember.
- **The ability is not freely controlled.** Death is required, checkpoints are not selected at will, and accumulated information remains incomplete. This prevents clean, costless optimization.
- **Psychological deterioration is real progression.** Subaru becomes more capable because he has learned, but also more frightened, controlling, isolated, and willing to undervalue himself.

A University of Washington study characterizes Subaru’s power as passive rather than heroic: he cannot choose to rewind without suffering death, and his real capability is to learn through painful trial and error. It also notes that prior deaths make him fear subsequent death more, not less. [[UW analysis](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/bitstreams/9c04741f-fe09-4e4d-a984-d59d02f72aad/download)] The anime cast likewise described Return by Death as carrying sadness and pain alongside the excitement of another attempt. [[Crunchyroll cast interview](https://www.crunchyroll.com/news/features/2016/5/1/rezero-cast-members-talk-characters-returning-by-death-more)]

#### Translation to Everquill

Everquill cannot copy the literal rewind: its world moves forward and its dead stay dead. It can, however, reproduce the **asymmetry of inherited knowledge**.

Subaru is effectively a living journal. Everquill externalizes that function:

- The former character experienced the truth.
- The journal records only what they noticed and chose to preserve.
- The heir receives only what survived and became accessible.
- The player may remember more than the heir, producing an important epistemic tension.
- The world remembers something different again, filtered through witnesses, interests, grief, and rumor.

That creates an even harsher version of Re:Zero’s inheritance: knowledge survives, but the person capable of interpreting it correctly may not.

The journal should therefore never behave like an omniscient quest log. It should be **valuable because it is situated, partial, personal, and expensive in lived experience**.

### Grimgar of Fantasy and Ash: ordinary lives make ordinary deaths matter

*Grimgar* earns its grief before and after death through mundanity:

- Food, lodging, weak equipment, minor injuries, and modest earnings matter.
- Even a goblin is a frightened living opponent capable of killing an inexperienced party.
- A core death comes from plausible carelessness and overconfidence, not cosmic destiny.
- The survivors continue living together after the funeral. Grief affects blame, leadership, competence, intimacy, and their ability to function.
- The dead character remains present through missing capabilities and altered group habits rather than supernatural cameos.

The series has been praised for centering mortality and mundanity: skipped meals, rusty equipment, and minor enemies all carry credible danger, while one avoidable death transforms the story into sustained reflection on grief. [[Otaku USA review](https://otakuusamagazine.com/grimgar-ashes-and-illusions-deluxe-edition-review-fatal-fantasies/)] Haruhiro’s later leadership is explicitly shaped by the person he lost. [[Crunchyroll feature](https://www.crunchyroll.com/th/news/features/2023/1/26/feature-grimgar-ashes-and-illusions-offers-a-sincere-depiction-of-grief)]

#### Translation to Everquill

Attachment should not depend solely on epic quests. A character needs ordinary texture:

- a favorite meal or habitual purchase;
- a landlord, trainer, sibling, drinking companion, rival, or animal;
- a recurring route through town;
- an unpaid debt or promised favor;
- a personal ritual before danger;
- one fear, one desire, and one contradiction.

After death, small absences should persist. A shopkeeper removes the dead character’s usual order from the stove. A rented room is cleared. An apprentice repeats a phrase incorrectly. A companion refuses a familiar road. This is often more powerful than a grand generated eulogy.

Unremarkable characters must be allowed unremarkable deaths. The game should not inflate every victim into “the realm’s greatest hero.” Specificity gives a modest life dignity; hyperbole makes the entire memorial system feel synthetic.

### Goblin Slayer: early lethality establishes the meaning of later danger

*Goblin Slayer* uses a novice party’s catastrophic first expedition to establish that supposedly trivial enemies are lethal when approached carelessly. The audience subsequently understands what goblins can do without every later encounter needing the same explicit brutality. Its setting is openly indebted to tabletop adventuring and dice-governed outcomes. [[Creator interview](https://www.crunchyroll.com/news/interviews/2018/10/30/goblin-slayer-creator-shares-his-love-for-western-fantasy); [Yen Press tabletop RPG](https://yenpress.com/titles/9781975318321-goblin-slayer-tabletop-roleplaying-game)]

The useful principle is:

> **One credible demonstration of danger can sustain tension longer than a high ongoing body count.**

The major caution is that shock can turn victims into props. If characters exist only to be brutalized so the setting looks serious, death establishes content severity but not emotional weight.

#### Translation to Everquill

The opening should establish that:

- low-status threats can kill;
- preparation, reconnaissance, retreat, and surrender are real actions;
- explicit free-text bravado does not invoke protagonist privilege;
- the referee will uphold declared risks.

It should not require a scripted player death or gratuitous suffering. A persistent corpse, a wounded survivor, an abandoned expedition, or a fully legible NPC disaster can teach the rule. The point is not “this world is sadistic”; it is “this world honors causality.”

### Overlord: power does not remove anxiety when beings are singular

*Overlord* provides an inversion. Ainz is overwhelmingly powerful and resurrection exists, yet the guardians of Nazarick carry exceptional emotional value because they are individual creations linked to absent guildmates. They are treated as family and as living remnants of relationships that cannot simply be reproduced. [[Japan Powered analysis](https://www.japanpowered.com/anime-articles/overlord)]

The design lesson is not that resurrection costs enough money. It is that **replaceability is social and historical, not statistical**. A mechanically equivalent guardian would not embody the same creator, shared past, or accumulated relationship.

#### Translation to Everquill

An heir may fill the same combat class without replacing the deceased. Preserve distinctions that cannot be rebuilt through grinding:

- unique handwriting and interpretation of events;
- specific promises and betrayals;
- private names used by friends;
- reputation with particular witnesses;
- knowledge that died unwritten;
- the exact moral terms on which alliances were formed;
- the deceased’s place in another person’s life.

Resurrection can restore a body and consciousness without restoring the world that person left.

## What legacy games teach

### Rogue Legacy: put something desirable immediately after death

The original *Rogue Legacy* deliberately moved RPG progression between lives. Its developers sought to make death fun without making it painless: permanent manor development meant the player did not restart from nothing, transparent mechanics reduced arbitrary loss, instant-death events were avoided, and short lives were followed by a streamlined successor flow. [[Rogue Legacy GDC presentation](https://media.gdcvault.com/GDC2014/Presentations/Lee_Teddy_Rogue_Legacy_Design.pdf)]

*Rogue Legacy 2* adds another important principle: successor traits should offer tradeoffs rather than obvious inferior choices. Difficult traits receive compensating incentives, encouraging experimentation. [[Nintendo interview](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/indie-world-interview-rogue-legacy-2-game-designer-teddy-lee-talks-about-the-newly-available-heir/)]

Everquill should not copy extensive stat inheritance, which would turn dead characters into upgrade currency. It should copy the pacing principle:

> **After grief, immediately reveal what this death has made newly possible to know, witness, choose, or confront.**

The reward is not “your family gained +5% damage.” It is a changed world, a new viewpoint, an accessible journal, a rumor reaching the wrong person, or an unfinished relationship now seen from the other side.

---

### Darkest Dungeon: vulnerability gives heroism contrast

*Darkest Dungeon* was designed against the romanticized, immutable RPG hero. Its creators emphasize flawed people, psychological vulnerability, permanent decisions, and incomplete information. [[Chris Bourassa interview](https://80.lv/articles/red-hook-studios-talks-about-the-creation-of-darkest-dungeon); [official overview](https://www.darkestdungeon.com/darkest-dungeon/about/)]

Its Graveyard records each fallen hero’s name, level, class, and circumstances of death. Resurrection exists only through a rare event with restrictions. [[Official Darkest Dungeon Wiki](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Graveyard)]

Two lessons transfer:

1. Memorials work when they preserve **concrete facts**, not generic praise.
2. Continued play needs a stable layer larger than the deceased—in *Darkest Dungeon*, the Hamlet; in Everquill, the world.

The caution is roster utilitarianism. When replacements arrive freely and frequently, players learn to treat inexperienced characters as disposable inputs. Everquill’s single-character heir structure makes this especially dangerous: each heir must be introduced as a protagonist with an existing life, not as a blank refill.

---

### XCOM: responsibility, specificity, and the savescumming problem

Jake Solomon argues that *XCOM* requires credible permadeath because success feels authentic only when loss was genuinely possible. Clear rules and visible odds are essential; turn-based control ensures the player does not blame an autonomous system for killing a soldier. [[PCGamesN interview](https://www.pcgamesn.com/xcom-2/xcom-jake-solomon-interview)]

Its memorial wall succeeds through restraint: name, portrait, kills, operation, location, and date, accompanied by a small ritual. These facts supply props from which players construct their own stories. [[Kotaku report](https://kotaku.com/remembering-the-fallen-and-the-decisions-for-which-the-5916627)]

The same report records Solomon reloading after a cherished colonel died. That is crucial evidence: savescumming is often not simple cowardice. It is a response to the conflict between attachment and an outcome the player cannot emotionally accept. Firaxis also rejected some harsher grief mechanics because losing the soldier was already sufficiently painful. [[XCOM grief design discussion](https://kotaku.com/xcom-2s-upcoming-expansion-is-inspired-by-shadow-of-mor-1796314016)]

#### Translation to Everquill

- The deterministic referee must expose the causal chain behind a fatal result.
- The player should see both likelihood and stakes: “70% success” is inadequate if the 30% failure branch means death.
- Free text must be converted into a visible mechanical interpretation before an irreversible roll whenever ambiguity is material.
- Canonical play should be append-only and automatically sealed, making reloads ineffective.
- A separate clearly noncanonical rehearsal or accessibility mode is preferable to weakening death secretly.
- Never shame players for wanting an undo. Treat frequent rollback attempts as evidence that the result felt opaque, disproportionate, or too costly in repeated time.

---

### Dwarf Fortress: play the world, not the doomed settlement

Tarn Adams describes “Losing is fun” as a way to create comfort with permadeath and encourages players to think of themselves as playing the world rather than one fortress. Fallen sites become future places to explore; an excellent defense in one mode can become a dangerous ruin in another. [[Tarn Adams interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/interview-the-making-of-dwarf-fortress)]

Its memorials record specific details such as year, cause, killer, family role, status, and personal preferences. [[Dwarf Fortress memorial documentation](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Memorial)]

Everquill’s strongest promise is a character-scale version of this idea:

> **Play the world, but experience it through people who remain fully mortal.**

The corpse, enemies, journals, abandoned hideout, unpaid debts, damaged village, and false stories about the death should become future content rather than archived flavor text.

## Design principles for weight without punishment

### 1. Death must be possible, not routine

“No plot armor” should govern outcomes, not mandate a body count. Death loses force when it becomes the expected end of every short session.

Use nonfatal consequences extensively:

- injury and permanent impairment;
- capture;
- forced retreat;
- loss of equipment or position;
- exposure of secrets;
- factional defeat;
- debt, disgrace, or separation;
- an ally dying instead;
- an enemy escaping stronger.

Real death should remain possible whenever the fiction and referee support it. It should not be the universal failure result.

### 2. Show consequence severity separately from success probability

Before an irreversible action, surface:

- interpreted intent;
- relevant known facts;
- dice expression and modifiers;
- success probability or risk band;
- likely failure categories;
- whether death is on the failure tree;
- whether retreat, surrender, preparation, or a safer formulation remains available.

Suggested language:

| Risk label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Guarded | Failure is plausible; fatal outcome not currently exposed |
| Perilous | Serious injury, capture, or major loss is exposed |
| Dire | Death is a credible result of failure |
| Desperate | Death is more likely than clean success |
| Suicidal | The declared action provides no credible survival path |

These labels must be calculated by the referee, never improvised by the narrator.

### 3. Preserve causal legibility after death

Every death needs an auditable chain:

> declared action → mechanical interpretation → known/unknown conditions → rolls → state changes → fatal injury → death

The player should be able to answer “Why did I die?” without relying on the LLM’s prose. Unknown dangers may exist, but their effects must still follow stable laws and leave discoverable evidence.

### 4. Never erase playtime; convert it into world history

A character may lose all portable power and still leave:

- changed faction relationships;
- saved or endangered people;
- discovered routes;
- slain or alerted enemies;
- debts and promises;
- caches and corpses;
- altered prices, laws, settlements, or ecosystems;
- journals and letters;
- rumors;
- heirs and rivals with reasons to act.

Not every reckless death deserves a material reward. Every death does deserve truthful incorporation into history.

### 5. Make heirs continuations of consequences, not continuations of builds

The heir should have their own:

- motive;
- competence;
- relationships;
- starting location;
- interpretation of the deceased;
- tolerance for inherited danger;
- reason to accept or reject unfinished business.

The heir may be the same class, but should never be presented as “the old character again at level one.”

### 6. Keep mourning expressive but mechanically light

Grief should produce changed behavior and content, not a compounding punishment tax.

Good consequences:

- a companion becomes unavailable briefly;
- dialogue and routines change;
- a funeral or body-recovery request appears;
- an ally becomes more cautious;
- a rival expresses guilt or contempt;
- a faction debates the deceased’s meaning.

Dangerous consequences:

- permanent global penalties after every death;
- unavoidable stat debuffs on each heir;
- mandatory long funeral sequences;
- grief triggering repeated uncontrollable death spirals;
- making every surviving friend functionally useless.

### 7. Specific facts outperform generated sentiment

A concise memorial entry such as:

> **Ilyra Fen, 23. Died at Glass Ford after holding the western stair while Neme and two ferrymen escaped. Body unrecovered. Journal last seen in her oilskin satchel.**

is stronger than several paragraphs calling her courageous, beloved, and unforgettable.

Let players supply the final interpretation through an epitaph, selected journal excerpt, or silence.

### 8. Restart at the world’s current narrative altitude

An heir should not repeat introductory rat hunts merely because the previous character died. They possess almost none of the predecessor’s power, but the world already contains developed conflicts and social structures.

Provide low-power ways to engage mature situations:

- investigation;
- negotiation;
- carrying messages;
- serving existing factions;
- recovering records;
- working through intermediaries;
- traveling to a less dangerous front;
- exploiting known routes or political divisions.

### 9. Prevent death from becoming optimal farming

Do not award guaranteed stat growth, premium currency, resurrection components, or stronger heirs simply for dying. This turns suicide into progression and converts characters into consumables.

Death may reveal new content because the world changed. That content should be consequence, not a payout.

### 10. Bind narration to the referee’s factual record

The LLM may choose voice, imagery, pacing, and emphasis. It must not:

- invent a heroic sacrifice the engine did not establish;
- claim witnesses who were absent;
- restore a lost journal for convenience;
- alter the killer or cause;
- manufacture final words;
- imply survival or resurrection;
- transform rumor into canonical truth;
- spare a favored character.

## Recommended heir flow

### Phase 1: the sealed death

The referee commits the fatal state before narration begins. The death record contains:

- character, date, and location;
- cause and responsible actor, if known;
- final declared action;
- exact mechanical resolution;
- witnesses;
- people demonstrably saved or harmed;
- body condition and location;
- possessions and journal disposition;
- unresolved obligations;
- factions affected;
- rumor seeds supported by observable facts.

The narrator then renders the final moment from facts the dying character could perceive. Avoid an automatic eloquent speech. Most deaths should be confused, abrupt, or unfinished.

### Phase 2: silence and factual reckoning

Present a short pause followed by a compact death-deed card. Offer:

- **Mourn now**
- **See what the world did next**
- **Choose the heir**

Mourning content remains permanently available, so skipping it is not treated as disrespect.

### Phase 3: the world pulse

Advance only the amount of time justified by discovery, communication, and heir arrival. Simulate:

- corpse discovery or continued exposure;
- enemy reactions;
- delivery or loss of messages;
- companion and family responses;
- completion or collapse of time-sensitive plans;
- initial rumor propagation.

Show only what the prospective heir or framing source could know. Keep an omniscient audit chronicle separately for players who want mechanical verification.

### Phase 4: three successor perspectives

When world state allows it, offer three qualitatively different candidates:

| Heir type | Likely access | Likely burden | Distinct value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kin** | Family letters, old stories, legal identity | Inherited enemies, grief, expectations | Recognition and intimate pre-adventure context |
| **Apprentice/associate** | Methods, professional contacts, shared plans | Duty, comparison, unfinished training | Understands how the deceased worked |
| **Finder/stranger** | Corpse, field journal, cached possessions | Little trust or legitimacy | Neutral reputation and potentially fuller physical evidence |

These are starting relationships, not bonuses. The apprentice’s skills are their own. The kin’s legitimacy is granted by living people. The finder owns only what they actually recovered.

Do not permit rerolling candidates until one appears mechanically superior. Declined candidates should remain in the world as NPCs where appropriate.

### Phase 5: stance toward the dead

After selection, ask for one nonbinding orientation:

- complete their work;
- avenge them;
- understand what happened;
- protect those left behind;
- clear the family name;
- reject their legacy;
- use the journal for an unrelated goal.

This establishes the new protagonist as a person making a decision, not an extension executing the player’s old quest list.

### Phase 6: enter through an event, not a menu

Open with the heir:

- receiving the death notice;
- recognizing a distorted tavern rumor;
- finding the corpse;
- attending or refusing the funeral;
- being questioned over the surname;
- reading the first relevant journal entry;
- meeting someone who loved or hated the deceased.

Target less than ninety seconds from death to the first meaningful heir decision unless the player chooses extended mourning.

## Mourning, memorial, deeds, and rumors

### Four layers of remembrance

#### 1. Physical memory

- corpse and wounds;
- grave, pyre, cairn, empty tomb, or no burial;
- surviving possessions;
- bloodstains or environmental damage;
- enemy trophies;
- abandoned homes and camps.

The memorial must not teleport the body. An empty marker and a recovered body are different world states.

#### 2. Private memory

Close NPCs remember specific experiences:

- a promise;
- an argument;
- a repeated joke;
- a meal;
- a failure;
- how the deceased treated them.

These memories affect behavior but need not all become quests.

#### 3. Civic memory

Organizations may create:

- guild death rolls;
- military plaques;
- temple prayers;
- legal inheritance records;
- wanted posters marked deceased;
- settlement memorials;
- enemy kill tallies.

The form depends on culture, reputation, and whether news arrived.

#### 4. Folk memory

Rumors propagate from witnesses and evidence. Each rumor should track:

- originating observation;
- speaker;
- audience;
- geographic and factional reach;
- confidence;
- mutations;
- current believers;
- contradiction by later evidence.

Keep three representations separate:

- **Canonical event:** what the referee resolved.
- **Recorded claim:** what a journal, witness, or institution says.
- **Public belief:** what a particular group currently accepts.

A deed such as “died saving the ferrymen” should require causal support: the character’s action materially enabled their survival. The LLM cannot award martyrdom through tone alone.

### Memorial content format

Each deceased character should receive:

- one factual death entry;
- portrait or personal mark;
- lifespan and places lived;
- cause, location, witnesses, and body status;
- three to five significant deeds, including failures where relevant;
- surviving relationships;
- one player-selected epitaph, journal fragment, or blank space;
- a rumor map showing how the story changed.

Avoid compulsory “achievement” language for every death. “Killed by a cellar rat after ignoring fever” can remain exactly that. Meaning may emerge later when someone finds the journal.

## Knowledge inheritance through journals

### The epistemic model

Everquill should track five distinct kinds of knowledge:

1. **World truth:** referee facts, potentially unknown to everyone.
2. **Character knowledge:** facts or claims the current character has legitimate access to.
3. **Written knowledge:** claims stored in journals, letters, maps, and records.
4. **Social knowledge:** memories and rumors held by specific NPCs or factions.
5. **Player memory:** information the human recalls but the current heir may not possess.

Free-text actions make this separation essential. If the player says, “I search the third crypt because that is where Vael hid the key,” the referee should determine whether the heir has a provenance for that knowledge. Without one, the heir may still choose the crypt, but cannot receive knowledge-based advantages or narrate certainty they do not possess.

### Journal entry structure

Each mechanical entry should contain:

- author;
- date and location;
- exact claim;
- whether it was observed, inferred, reported, or suspected;
- source or witness;
- confidence;
- linked people, places, objects, and obligations;
- whether corroborated by later evidence;
- physical copy and current location;
- encryption, damage, or alteration status;
- a separate player annotation if desired.

The narrator may turn this into prose, but the structured claim remains authoritative for knowledge access.

### Low-friction capture

Do not require the player to manually document every clue.

After important scenes:

1. The referee proposes two to five mechanically grounded facts.
2. The player selects which the character records.
3. The player may dictate or type a personal interpretation.
4. The journal stores factual claims and personal prose separately.

Support voice journaling, copying, caching, sending letters, and sharing selected pages. This makes preservation an intentional practice without turning the game into clerical labor.

### Loss and recovery

A journal is a world object:

- carried journals may remain with the corpse;
- cached copies remain where placed;
- sent letters may reach recipients;
- companions may recover or steal pages;
- fire, water, and deliberate destruction follow deterministic rules;
- hostile actors may read or alter accessible material.

However, journal destruction must be legible and telegraphed. Randomly deleting dozens of hours of notes produces resentment rather than tragedy.

The best protection is redundancy chosen through play:

- field journal;
- home copy;
- letters;
- apprentice’s notes;
- institutional archive;
- encoded emergency cache.

### Reading as interpretation, not automatic absorption

On acquiring a large journal, the heir should first see:

- five most actionable leads;
- entries concerning the death;
- named living contacts;
- known threats;
- unresolved promises;
- contradictions and uncertain claims.

The full journal remains searchable. Reading time can matter in dangerous situations, but should not require opening every page to unlock knowledge the heir plausibly studied during downtime.

### Prevent omniscience

A journal may be wrong. If the deceased wrote “Chancellor Orun is a vampire,” the heir inherits the claim, not the truth. The UI should distinguish:

- confirmed observation;
- secondhand report;
- inference;
- suspicion;
- deliberate player-authored statement.

This allows lies, paranoia, manipulation, and mistaken theories without letting narration overwrite the deterministic world.

## Resurrection without cheapening death

Resurrection should be introduced as a known law early, but remain an endgame accomplishment. Secretly revealing later that everyone could have been revived would retroactively weaken mourning.

Use several deterministic component laws with multiple difficult substitutes:

1. **Identity law:** true name plus corpse, blood relation, signature artifact, or equivalent identity anchor.
2. **Memory law:** journal, intimate witness constellation, preserved soul impression, or other continuity evidence.
3. **Vessel law:** viable remains or a lawful replacement body.
4. **Passage law:** unique site, time, ritual authority, or threshold condition.
5. **Power law:** an endgame-scale, consumed resource that cannot be mass-farmed.

Requirements must be inspectable. If a route is impossible, state which law fails. Do not add a hidden resurrection percentage after the player assembles the correct components.

Successful resurrection must not rewind history:

- the heir’s life and deeds remain real;
- the resurrected character returns after elapsed time;
- relationships have changed;
- possessions have changed hands;
- enemies remember the death;
- the corpse’s treatment matters;
- rumors and political consequences persist;
- unfinished business may have become impossible or irrelevant.

The resurrected character should not automatically replace the heir. The player may eventually decide whom to follow, while the other remains a consequential person. Resurrection should produce a difficult reunion, not restore an old save.

## Principal failure modes and safeguards

| Failure mode | What it teaches the player | Safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| **Repeated early grind** | Death deletes time | Restart at current world altitude; provide alternative low-power engagement |
| **Opaque free-text adjudication** | The narrator can kill arbitrarily | Confirm mechanical interpretation before irreversible rolls |
| **Disposable heirs** | Characters are ammunition | Give every heir existing ties, motives, and a distinct entry perspective |
| **Death farming** | Suicide is efficient | No guaranteed power or premium currency from death |
| **Savescumming pressure** | Canon is less valuable than attachment | Transparent stakes, append-only outcomes, separate noncanonical rehearsal |
| **Journal busywork** | Knowledge preservation is a chore | Automatic factual candidates plus optional personal annotation |
| **Journal omniscience** | Writing makes claims true | Track observation, source, confidence, and contradiction |
| **Meta-knowledge abuse** | Heirs are skins over one omniscient player | Require in-world provenance for knowledge-based advantages |
| **Death spiral** | One loss makes the campaign unwinnable | No global scaling by death count; preserve refuge, relocation, diplomacy, and counter-factions |
| **Memorial inflation** | Every death receives the same artificial praise | Specific facts, culturally contingent rituals, optional silence |
| **Grief taxation** | Caring makes future play weaker | Behavioral and narrative mourning with capped mechanical penalties |
| **Cheap resurrection** | Death is deferred inconvenience | Known component laws, endgame scarcity, no history rewind |
| **Resurrection lottery** | The game arbitrarily chooses whose grief matters | Player-directed lawful process, never random selection |
| **Exploitative shock** | Victims exist to establish tone | Build ordinary attachment and credible causality before brutality |
| **Rumor spam** | World memory is notification noise | Surface only rumors that reach relevant people or change available action |
| **LLM hagiography** | Facts bend toward drama | Separate referee truth, recorded claims, rumor, and narration |
| **Corpse-run repetition** | Every heir must retrace the same route | Make recovery one option among burial, copying, witness testimony, and abandonment |
| **Overlethality** | Attachment is irrational | Use injury, capture, retreat, and social failure more often than death |

## Validation criteria

Playtests should measure whether the system produces regret and authorship rather than blame and disengagement.

- At least 90% of testers can accurately explain the mechanical cause of their death.
- Players understand before acting when death is exposed, even if exact hidden information remains unknown.
- Median time from death to the heir’s first meaningful decision is under ninety seconds.
- Heirs are described as different people, not “the same build again.”
- Players revisit memorials or journals voluntarily rather than because rewards require it.
- Journal use produces decisions; it does not merely restate the quest log.
- Players can distinguish referee truth, the deceased’s belief, and public rumor.
- Repeated deaths do not force repeated tutorial content or make the world mathematically unplayable.
- Death frequency does not rise merely because the player has previously survived for a long time.
- Players who reject a death primarily cite emotional loss, not ambiguity, bugs, or misunderstood mechanics.
- The most remembered deaths include modest, accidental, and unresolved deaths—not only heroic sacrifices.
- Resurrecting someone feels like meeting a person displaced by history, not loading an earlier character sheet.

## Final design position

Everquill’s promise should be:

> **The dice will not save you. The narrator will not save you. But the world will not erase you.**

Re:Zero demonstrates that retained knowledge can make each death heavier when emotional cost accumulates. *Grimgar* shows that ordinary routines and continued grief make modest lives matter. *Goblin Slayer* demonstrates how credible early lethality sustains later tension. *Overlord* shows that singular relationships create irreplaceability even where resurrection and overwhelming power exist. *Rogue Legacy* makes the next beginning immediate; *Darkest Dungeon* preserves vulnerability and records the fallen; *XCOM* connects clear responsibility to attachment; *Dwarf Fortress* turns ruins and failure into future history.

Everquill should combine them by making every character singular, every fatal result legible, every journal partial, every rumor interested, every heir distinct, and every death a forward-moving wound in the same world.
